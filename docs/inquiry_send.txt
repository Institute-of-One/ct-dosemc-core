Subject: Pre-submission inquiry - Paper (not Note): what limits index-based organ dose in abdominal CT

--- everything below this line is the message body ---

Dear Editor,

I would like to ask whether the following study is appropriate as a Paper, rather than a Note, for the Journal of Radiological Protection, and whether its subject sits within the journal's scope.

Title. Between-patient variation sets the limit on index-based organ dose in abdominal CT: a cross-validated bound on five conversions.

Question. Organ dose is routinely estimated from the volume CT dose index by a conversion - a single factor, a size correction, a dose-length-product factor, or a per-organ coefficient library. How close such a conversion can get to the dose that transport actually deposits, in a real patient rather than a reference phantom, has not been measured on the same anatomy. This study measures it.

Method. A GPU Monte-Carlo pipeline computes absorbed organ dose in patient anatomy from routine acquisition metadata alone. The transport engine is verified against Case 4 of AAPM Task Group 195: at the central volume of interest it differs from PENELOPE by 0.359%, with an uncertainty measured from ten independent replicas rather than assumed. On 30 multi-vendor abdominal CT series from a public archive - 342 organ-series pairs across 12 organs - five conversions of increasing richness are scored under leave-one-patient-out cross-validation, so that every error reported is on a patient the conversion was not fitted on.

Results. A single conversion factor for the whole cohort recovers absorbed organ dose to a median 31%. One factor per organ, which is what a coefficient library offers, does not improve on it: 32%. Adding a scan-length term helps, and adding either the organ or the patient's size on top of that changes little; the best of the five reaches a median 13%. The reason is in the variance: 88% of the variance of the dose-to-index ratio is between patients and 6% is between organs, so a table indexed by organ is refining the smaller term.

Why I am asking. The finding that matters to a reader is negative - a per-organ coefficient table does not repay the effort - and negative findings are sometimes read as slight. The work is a full study rather than a single observation: a verified engine with a measured uncertainty, a pre-declared eligibility gate, a cohort with every exclusion counted, five conversions compared under cross-validation, and a variance decomposition that explains the result. At 6,000 words with six figures and one table it is within the 8,500-word equivalent the journal allows a Paper, and well beyond what a 2,500-word Note can carry.

I should also be clear about what the study does not do, since the journal's readership will ask. The quantity is absorbed organ dose, not risk. A risk statement would need age and sex, which the public archive does not supply, and a risk model, which is not used here. The paper says so.

The implementation, the drivers that produced every number, the recorded design with its amendments, and the measured values as data files are public and archived with a DOI; the manuscript is generated from those files, so no number in it is typed by hand. No imaging is redistributed.

I would be grateful for your view on the article type and the fit.
