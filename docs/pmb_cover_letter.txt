Dear Editors,

Please consider the enclosed Paper, "A measured floor on index-based organ dose estimation in CT, where per-organ coefficients do not improve on a single conversion factor", for publication in Physics in Medicine & Biology.

The volume CT dose index is a scanner output rather than a patient dose, and the field converts it to organ dose with size corrections, dose-length-product factors and libraries of per-organ coefficients. How close any such conversion can get has not, to my knowledge, been measured against transport on the same anatomy. This paper measures that floor. A GPU Monte-Carlo pipeline computes absorbed organ dose in each patient's own segmented, density-mapped anatomy from routine metadata alone; the engine is verified against Case 4 of AAPM Task Group 195 to 0.359 per cent of PENELOPE, or 1.67 combined standard deviations, against a yardstick fixed before the comparison. On 30 multi-vendor abdominal series from a public archive, 342 organ-series pairs across 12 organs, conversions of increasing richness are scored under leave-one-patient-out cross-validation. A single factor for the cohort recovers absorbed organ dose to a median 31 per cent; one factor per organ does not improve on it; adding a scan-length term reaches 16 per cent; and adding either organ identity or patient size reaches a floor of 13 per cent. Eighty-eight per cent of the variance in the index-to-dose ratio lies between patients and six per cent between organs, while the Monte-Carlo statistical uncertainty is 0.012 per cent of the dose at the median, so the residual is physical rather than a sampling artefact.

The result is a negative one stated positively: the information an index withholds is a property of the patient, not of the organ, so a per-organ coefficient table cannot supply it and neither can a size correction. I have tried to keep the claim inside what the data support. The paper does not claim novelty for GPU Monte-Carlo photon transport, for spectrum modelling, for Hounsfield-unit-to-density calibration or for deep-learning organ segmentation; each is used as published and cited. What is new is the measurement of the ceiling, and the decomposition that says where the missing information lives.
