Dear Editor,

I would like to confirm that the following manuscript is appropriate as a Paper,
rather than a Note, for Physics in Medicine and Biology.

Title. A measured floor on index-based organ dose estimation in CT, where per-organ
coefficients do not improve on a single conversion factor.

The question. CTDIvol is a scanner output and not a patient dose, and the field
converts it toward organ dose in three established ways: a size correction, a
dose-length-product factor, and libraries of per-organ coefficients computed once on
reference phantoms. Each replaces transport through this patient with a coefficient
obtained from someone else's. How close such a conversion can get has not, to my
knowledge, been measured against transport on the same anatomy. That is what this
manuscript measures.

I am not claiming that an index and an absorbed dose are the same quantity, nor that
anyone believes they are. The contribution is the size and the structure of what is
left over.

Approach. A GPU Monte-Carlo pipeline computes absorbed organ dose from routine
metadata, through each patient's own segmented and density-mapped anatomy, with every
dose coefficient computed rather than looked up. The engine is verified against AAPM
TG-195 Case 4 against a yardstick fixed before any comparison was run, with a
replica-measured rather than assumed uncertainty: at the centre volume of interest
|ours - PENELOPE| = 0.359%, or 1.67 combined standard deviations, and the phantom
length term is measured and null. All four published codes are shown, so that the
choice of yardstick is auditable rather than convenient.

On 30 multi-vendor abdominal series from a public archive - 342 organ-series pairs
over 12 organs - I then fit conversions of increasing richness from an organ-weighted
CTDIvol index to the absorbed dose, and score each under leave-one-patient-out
cross-validation.

Main results. Held out, on patients the fit never saw:

  a single conversion factor for the cohort     median 31%   90th percentile 75%
  one factor per organ                          median 32%   90th percentile 68%
  a scan-length term                            median 16%   90th percentile 39%
  scan length and a per-organ offset            median 13%   90th percentile 36%
  scan length and patient size                  median 13%   90th percentile 32%

Two of these are the paper. A per-organ coefficient - which is the form in which
published organ-dose coefficient libraries are used - does not improve on a single
number for the whole cohort; cross-validated it is slightly worse, because twelve
organ means fitted on twenty-nine patients do not transfer to the thirtieth. And the
floor is reached at a median of 13%: nothing an index carries closes the remaining
gap.

The variance decomposition says why. 88% of the variance in the log index-to-dose
ratio lies between patients, 6% between organs and 4% between vendors. Within one
patient a single number converts the index to absorbed dose for every one of that
patient's organs to within a factor of 1.18; across patients that number spans 0.88
to 4.79. The information an index withholds is a property of the patient, nearly
common to all of that patient's organs at once, and a per-organ coefficient cannot
supply it. Patient size, the standard patient-level correction, does not carry it
either: its correlation with the patient-level factor is r = -0.30.

Per-organ Monte-Carlo statistical uncertainty is 0.012% of the dose at the median, so
the residual is more than three orders of magnitude above the sampling noise. It is
physical.

One point I would rather state than have inferred. The scan-length term is not
offered as a discovery: CTDIvol is defined per rotation and carries no length,
dose-length product is the standard quantity that does, and the fitted slope on
log n_z is 1.14, close enough to one that the dependence is largely accumulation. It
is included because a conversion that omitted it would be a straw man, and its slope
is reported in the manuscript so a reader can judge that.

Why this is a Paper. PMB defines Papers as original scientific research covering
techniques and applications, and the manuscript has both: a verification with a
measured uncertainty budget and a pre-declared yardstick, and a cross-validated
measurement of a limit on a class of methods in wide clinical use.

Why this is not a Note. A Note would be the engine, or the TG-195 reproduction, on
its own. Those are Methods here. The claim is a measured floor and a decomposition of
what sits beneath it.

Related work, and why this is not salami. Two earlier papers from this line report
different endpoints on the same pipeline: whole-scan CTDIvol
(doi:10.5281/zenodo.21636719, v0.1.1) and an organ-weighted CTDIvol index
(doi:10.5281/zenodo.21817307, v0.1.1). This manuscript reports absorbed dose in mGy
by Monte-Carlo transport, and uses it to measure how far the index endpoint can be
pushed. Three endpoints, one pipeline.

Length and limitations. Within the Paper limit: about 4 000 words of body, six
figures and one table. The cohort is 30 series with six to nine per vendor, which is
adequate for the patient-level question and inadequate for a vendor-level one; no
vendor claim is made, and the per-vendor table is descriptive.

If the Board would prefer a different article type, I would rather hear that now than
after review.
